Evidence-language convention
----------------------------
“Design contract” describes intended or implemented behavior, not a measured
performance result. “Measured exploratory” means the number is backed by a
recorded run but is not yet a paper-scale result. “Pending” means the result
must not be presented as validated. Preserve these distinctions while speaking.


SLIDE 01 — MosaicKV: Query-Forecasted Inter-Modal KV-Cache Compression
Purpose: Introduce the system and set an appropriately careful claim boundary.

Speaker notes:
Today I will present MosaicKV, a training-free system for compressing the
multimodal key-value cache during vision-language model inference. The central
idea is that cache entries should not be ranked independently using only past
attention. Instead, MosaicKV forecasts future query demand, represents
cross-modal evidence relationships, considers both keys and values, and retains
a repair path for uncertain decoding decisions. I will separate the design and
implementation from the small exploratory measurements. The latter demonstrate
behavior and expose failure modes, but they are not yet final paper claims.

Transition: I will first define the problem, then build the design component by
component, and finally show comparative and systems evidence.


SLIDE 02 — Talk Roadmap
Purpose: Explain the structure of the presentation and the evidence labels.

Speaker notes:
The talk has three parts. First, I will explain why multimodal KV caches are a
systems problem and why ordinary independent pruning is a scientific problem.
Second, I will describe MosaicKV’s forecasting, evidence graph, value-aware
selection, three-tier representation, and residual repair. Third, I will show
baseline comparisons, ablations, systems measurements, and limitations. The
labels are important: blue identifies design or implementation contracts, green
identifies provenance-backed exploratory measurements, and red identifies
pending evidence that should not enter a paper table.

Transition: To see where compression is possible, start with how the cache is
created and consumed.


SLIDE 03 — How a Multimodal KV Cache Is Created and Reused
Purpose: Give the minimum VLM and attention background needed for the talk.

Speaker notes:
The processor converts text and media into a joint sequence. During prefill,
each transformer layer and KV head projects hidden states into keys and values.
During every subsequent decoding step, the new query attends to this retained
prefix and a new key-value pair is appended. The important asymmetry is that the
prefix is produced once but read repeatedly. A mistaken eviction can therefore
affect every future token, not just the first token after compression. This is
why a cache compressor must preserve both addressability through K and the
attention output carried by V.

Transition: The repeated use of this state becomes expensive as multimodal
prefixes grow.


SLIDE 04 — The Systems Problem: Multimodal Prefixes Are Large
Purpose: Quantify what controls KV memory and state the systems objective.

Speaker notes:
KV storage grows linearly with precision, layer count, KV-head count, head
dimension, and sequence length. Multimodal sequence length includes text,
visual patches, OCR and layout tokens, and video frames. Multi-image and video
requests can therefore exceed the memory capacity that would otherwise support
larger batches or more concurrent requests. Our primary systems target is
measured active KV bytes. A reduction matters only if the resulting attention
working set saves more time than forecasting, selection, and cache packing cost.

Transition: Memory alone is not the whole challenge, because multimodal
evidence is not a collection of independent tokens.


SLIDE 05 — The Scientific Problem: Evidence Is Heterogeneous and Coupled
Purpose: Motivate structured evidence preservation.

Speaker notes:
Consider a chart question asking which bar is largest. The answer may require a
visual mark, a legend color, and an OCR axis label together. One element can have
low prompt-time attention while remaining essential to the reasoning chain. An
independent top-k rule can remove that weak-looking connector and destroy the
answer. Evidence can also become relevant only later in decoding, or matter
because its value vector changes the attention output even when its key receives
moderate attention. MosaicKV therefore treats compression as evidence-set
selection rather than isolated token ranking.

Transition: These observations lead to both algorithmic and systems
requirements.


SLIDE 06 — Research Problem and Design Requirements
Purpose: State the optimization problem and non-negotiable constraints.

Speaker notes:
The input is a full multimodal cache, an exact active-byte budget, and unknown
future decoder queries. The output is a smaller cache state that preserves
answer quality and ideally lowers decoding cost. Algorithmically, we need to
anticipate future demand, cover related evidence, account for keys and values,
and recover from selected mistakes. From the systems side, we need strict byte
accounting, correct logical and RoPE positions, deterministic identity at full
retention, and a favorable break-even point. We compare methods by realized
active bytes, not nominal token-retention percentages.

Transition: Before presenting MosaicKV, I will locate it relative to the main
controls and published methods.


SLIDE 07 — Baseline Landscape
Purpose: Introduce every baseline and its main limitation.

Speaker notes:
FullKV is the quality and systems reference. Random and uniform retention test
whether an apparently intelligent selector beats simple allocation under the
same byte budget. Prompt Attention Top-k is the strongest simple online control.
LOOK-M adds a text prior and pivot-based merging. PrefixKV allocates retention
by an offline layer profile. The ICLR VL-Cache method uses attention sparsity and
modality-aware, layer-adaptive pruning. MosaicKV combines forecast, graph, K/V
utility, prototypes, and repair, but pays richer planning overhead. The methods
ending in “reimpl” are local paper-faithful reimplementations, never official
code.

Transition: LOOK-M is the closest baseline in its use of KV merging, so I will
contrast it first.


SLIDE 08 — Baseline Deep Dive: LOOK-M vs. MosaicKV
Purpose: Distinguish pivot merging from the full MosaicKV design.

Speaker notes:
Our LOOK-M reimplementation follows prompt self-attention scoring, a text prior,
recent and high-score preservation, cosine-similar pivot assignment, and its
documented merge rules. MosaicKV differs at every decision boundary: it includes
temporary future queries, uses typed blocks and graph context, enforces exact
byte budgets, permits only phase-safe clusters, and preserves indexed residuals
for recovery. LOOK-M is a one-shot prompt-time merge method. MosaicKV treats
merging as one optional representation tier inside a dynamic and recoverable
state. LOOK-M’s documented LLaVA-1.5 MHA assumptions also limit fair model
coverage.

Transition: PrefixKV and VL-Cache differ in a second way: their budget is
calibrated offline rather than planned per request.


SLIDE 09 — Baseline Deep Dive: PrefixKV and VL-Cache vs. MosaicKV
Purpose: Compare information signals, budget shape, granularity, and recovery.

Speaker notes:
PrefixKV derives a per-layer profile from a prompt-attention CDF and calibration
search. VL-Cache measures attention sparsity and modality importance to assign a
layer-adaptive budget. Both retain or evict exact positions and have no online
recovery path. MosaicKV plans a block budget for the current request using
forecasted attention, K/V utility, and graph coverage, then may represent
compatible unselected blocks with prototypes and residuals. When PrefixKV is
applied outside its official model assumptions, we explicitly call it
generalized PrefixKV. Realized bytes and disclosed mandatory policies remain the
comparison axis.

Transition: Sophisticated methods are meaningful only if they also beat simple,
well-controlled baselines.


SLIDE 10 — Why Simple Baselines Still Matter
Purpose: Explain the role of random, uniform, and prompt-attention controls.

Speaker notes:
Random KV checks whether a ranking actually outperforms deterministic chance.
Uniform KV checks whether adaptive allocation across layers, heads, and
modalities is useful. Prompt Top-k isolates the incremental value of forecast,
graph structure, and value-aware utility over current attention alone. These are
not throwaway baselines: they use the same block size, mandatory-token policy,
byte budget, decode loop, and deterministic generation. They do not receive
prototype merging or repair, because adding those features would no longer test
the named baseline.

Transition: With those controls defined, the next table summarizes MosaicKV’s
structural novelty.


SLIDE 11 — What Is Structurally Different About MosaicKV?
Purpose: Summarize the feature-level differences in one table.

Speaker notes:
No comparison method combines all six columns. Existing methods mainly score
past attention and perform one-shot pruning or merging. MosaicKV exact adds
future-query forecasting, graph coverage, value-aware utility, and a submodular
block budget. MosaicKV proto adds a phase-safe representation of compatible
unselected evidence. MosaicKV full adds online residual repair. The hypothesis
is not simply that more mechanisms are better. It is that a compact cache should
jointly represent predicted demand, evidence coverage, value contribution, and
recoverability instead of optimizing one prompt-time scalar.

Transition: Here is that hypothesis as a single end-to-end system.


SLIDE 12 — MosaicKV in One Slide
Purpose: Establish the six-component mental model.

Speaker notes:
MosaicKV begins with the full prefetched cache. It estimates the query
distribution that will consume the cache, builds a sparse graph among evidence
blocks, computes value-aware utility, and selects blocks under an exact budget.
Selected blocks stay exact. Compatible unselected evidence may be summarized by
prototypes, while original sources can be retained in a CPU residual tier.
During decoding, uncertainty can trigger a bounded restoration. These six
mechanisms form the system contract; the next slides unpack them in execution
order.

Transition: First, I will trace one request through the complete pipeline.


SLIDE 13 — End-to-End Compression Pipeline
Purpose: Show ordering and state separation.

Speaker notes:
We prefill exactly once, derive modality spans, and blockize the full cache.
Forecasting then estimates future queries; the graph and utility modules turn
the cache into a selection problem; lazy greedy produces the active set. The
runtime gathers exact blocks, optionally constructs prototypes, and stores
residual sources before explicit token-by-token decoding. Three lengths remain
separate: the packed physical cache length, the original logical sequence
length, and the next decode position. This separation is essential for RoPE and
attention-mask correctness.

Transition: The first concrete data structure is the typed cache block.


SLIDE 14 — Component 0: Typed Blockization and Logical Positions
Purpose: Explain the common unit of selection and accounting.

Speaker notes:
MosaicKV partitions the sequence into fixed-size token blocks independently for
every layer and KV head. A descriptor records physical indices, original logical
positions, modality, token IDs when available, media metadata, dtype, device,
byte size, and whether the block is mandatory. Blocks never cross layer or head
boundaries. Although selected blocks may be packed densely, their logical
positions remain monotonic and unchanged. This gives every method the same
granularity and makes exact active-byte accounting possible.

Transition: Once the candidate blocks exist, we estimate which ones future
decoder queries will need.


SLIDE 15 — Component 1: Future-Query Forecasting Modes
Purpose: Explain prompt-window, draft-rollout, and hybrid forecasts.

Speaker notes:
Prompt-window mode summarizes queries from the last w prompt positions. It is
cheap but cannot observe continuation behavior. Draft-rollout mode reuses the
original prefill, performs r temporary greedy decode steps, records their query
vectors, and then discards the draft tokens. Hybrid mode combines prompt
statistics with normalized draft centroids and weights. The draft uses a cloned
cache, so it cannot mutate the cache used by the final run. True future queries
exist only in an evaluation-only oracle used to diagnose forecast quality.

Transition: The output is not a predicted string; it is a distribution over
future attention demand.


SLIDE 16 — Why Forecasting Changes the Selection Problem
Purpose: Connect forecast vectors to block-level attention estimates.

Speaker notes:
Prompt and draft queries are reduced to normalized centroids with explicit
weights. Each centroid is compared with the pooled key descriptor of every
block, and the weighted softmax mass becomes forecasted attention. Selection is
therefore conditioned on likely continuation queries rather than only on the
last observed prompt attention. We diagnose this approximation with cosine
similarity to true future queries, attention-rank correlation, selected-block
recall, and forecast regret. The oracle measures forecast error; it never informs
the online result.

Transition: Forecasted demand identifies likely blocks, but it does not yet
capture evidence relationships.


SLIDE 17 — Component 2: Sparse Cross-Modal Evidence Graph
Purpose: Explain graph nodes, edge semantics, and scalable storage.

Speaker notes:
Every node is a typed KV block. Edges can encode semantic similarity,
prompt-side co-activation, spatial adjacency, OCR or layout relations, temporal
adjacency, shared regions, and explicit cross-modal alignment. A chart mark can
therefore connect to a legend and OCR label; video evidence can connect across
nearby frames. We keep only top-d neighbors and compute similarity in chunks, so
normal execution stores sparse COO or CSR rather than an n-squared dense graph.
This graph supplies coverage and redundancy information to selection.

Transition: Not every plausible edge or cluster is valid, so compatibility is
enforced before scoring.


SLIDE 18 — Graph Compatibility Prevents Invalid Evidence Mixing
Purpose: Describe fail-closed graph and merge constraints.

Speaker notes:
Edges and prototype assignments may be restricted to the same layer and KV
head, approved modality pairs, bounded logical spans, and valid image, page, or
frame metadata. If structured metadata is unavailable, we do not invent it. The
fallback combines semantic top-d neighbors with local positional neighbors and
preserves mandatory boundaries, while recording which edge sources are missing.
We also report graph size, degree, connected components, modality mixing, and
evidence-cluster coverage so an expensive but uninformative graph is visible.

Transition: The graph now lets the utility function value both individual
blocks and their substitutability.


SLIDE 19 — Component 3: Value-Aware Block Utility
Purpose: Explain why keys, values, coverage, and redundancy all matter.

Speaker notes:
For each block we combine forecasted attention, expected attention-output
contribution, novelty relative to graph neighbors, coverage, modality rarity,
redundancy, and mandatory priority. Attention mass measures how strongly a key
may be addressed, but not what its value contributes or whether a neighbor can
replace it. The equation shown follows the registered coefficient convention in
the implementation; the important operational point is that contribution and
novelty are preservation rewards after applying those signs and weights. The
ablation later tests both logit and attention-output fidelity.

Transition: Utility scores become a constrained set-selection problem rather
than an unconstrained ranking.


SLIDE 20 — Component 4: Budgeted Submodular Selection
Purpose: Explain mandatory-first lazy greedy selection and exact budgets.

Speaker notes:
Mandatory blocks are selected first. Remaining candidates enter a lazy marginal-
gain heap, and the top candidate is recomputed before acceptance. Fixed-size
blocks use a cardinality budget; variable-size paths check exact bytes. Stable
block identifiers provide deterministic tie-breaking, and every selected block
records its marginal gain and reason. Facility and modality terms encourage
coverage instead of repeated near-duplicates. For tiny graphs, exhaustive search
measures the greedy optimality gap, and monotonicity or diminishing-return tests
run only where the objective assumptions actually hold.

Transition: Selection determines the anchors; the cache representation decides
what to do with the remaining evidence.


SLIDE 21 — Component 5: Three-Tier Cache State
Purpose: Introduce exact, prototype, and residual storage and budget semantics.

Speaker notes:
Selected high-utility blocks remain unchanged in the exact tier. Compatible
unselected blocks may be summarized by prototypes that remain active on the
GPU. Their original sources can be indexed in a CPU residual tier for possible
restoration. The primary serving budget counts exact plus prototype GPU bytes.
Residual CPU bytes and transfer traffic are always reported separately, so the
method cannot claim a misleading retention ratio by hiding a large residual
store.

Transition: The exact tier is the universal path and the basis of retention-one
identity.


SLIDE 22 — Exact Tier: The Universal Safety Path
Purpose: Define selection-only MosaicKV and its invariants.

Speaker notes:
Exact-tier construction gathers or aliases selected blocks without changing any
key or value. The packed tensors travel with their original logical positions
and the correct next decode position. This produces mosaickv_exact: forecast,
graph, utility, and budgeted selection, but no merge and no repair. Mandatory
blocks remain exact, storage-derived byte counts must match the reported bytes,
and full retention reconstructs the original cache exactly. If prototype
geometry is not audited for a model, this is the safe fallback rather than an
invalid approximation.

Transition: Prototype compression is more aggressive, but RoPE makes naive
averaging unsafe.


SLIDE 23 — Prototype Tier: Compression Must Respect RoPE Geometry
Purpose: Explain phase-safe prototype construction and rejection behavior.

Speaker notes:
Keys at different logical positions can carry different rotary phases. Averaging
post-RoPE keys directly can produce a vector that corresponds to no meaningful
position. A valid prototype path must move compatible keys to a common anchor
frame, compute weighted K and V prototypes in FP32, cast back to the cache dtype,
and associate the result with the anchor position. Clusters remain within one
layer and head and obey modality and span constraints. If an audited inverse and
re-anchoring transform is unavailable, merging is disabled and the runtime uses
exact selection only.

Transition: The residual tier preserves the source detail that prototypes may
lose.


SLIDE 24 — Residual Tier: A Recoverable Representation of Evicted Evidence
Purpose: Explain residual indexing, placement, and accounting.

Speaker notes:
For each prototype, MosaicKV can preserve the original source K/V blocks on CPU,
indexed by layer, head, prototype identifier, and logical position. The initial
safe representation is lossless FP16 or BF16, with optional lower-precision
storage studied separately. Pinned memory enables asynchronous transfer when the
platform supports it; pageable placement remains explicit. Residual storage is
not active GPU memory, but it is still real memory and must be reported. At full
retention, no prototype or residual transformation occurs at all.

Transition: During decoding, uncertainty signals decide whether any residual
detail should return to the exact tier.


SLIDE 25 — Component 6: Decode-Time Uncertainty Signals
Purpose: Define entropy, prototype risk, and optional KL triggers.

Speaker notes:
At a provisional decode step, normalized token entropy measures output
instability. Prototype risk multiplies current attention mass on a prototype by
its dispersion, targeting compressed clusters that are both influential and
heterogeneous. A cheap draft-distribution KL can be recorded or used only by a
policy that explicitly names it. The online policies are none, entropy,
prototype risk, and their disjunction. Oracle repair is evaluation-only and is
isolated from online compression.

Transition: A trigger starts a bounded state transaction, not an unbounded retry
loop.


SLIDE 26 — Residual Repair Is a Bounded Transaction
Purpose: Walk through one repair event and its safeguards.

Speaker notes:
The runtime first produces provisional logits. If the policy triggers, it ranks
prototype clusters by attention mass times dispersion, transfers at most R
source blocks, promotes them to exact blocks, refreshes the affected prototype,
and recomputes the current step once. Promoted blocks remain exact for later
steps. If promotion would exceed the budget, the lowest-utility non-promoted
prototype is evicted. Every event records the reason, positions, modalities,
bytes, transfer and re-decode times, logit and token changes, and whether quality
was recovered.

Transition: These components form three named runtime methods for controlled
ablation.


SLIDE 27 — Three Runtime Methods Share One Pipeline
Purpose: Define mosaickv_exact, mosaickv_proto, and mosaickv_full.

Speaker notes:
All three methods share forecast, graph, selector, mandatory policy, explicit
decode, and byte accounting. mosaickv_exact retains selected blocks exactly.
mosaickv_proto adds phase-safe prototypes when supported. mosaickv_full adds the
residual tier and decode-time repair. Unsupported prototype geometry causes an
explicit exact fallback; it must not masquerade as a successful prototype row.
At retention one, all method names take the same transformation-free FullKV path
within the documented numerical tolerance.

Transition: That identity rule is one member of a larger correctness contract.


SLIDE 28 — Correctness Invariants Are Part of the Design
Purpose: Emphasize correctness as a first-class research contribution.

Speaker notes:
Compression is invalid if cache ownership, byte accounting, positions, or
request isolation are wrong, even when generated text looks plausible. The
cache invariants enforce unique block ownership, non-conflicting tiers, exact
storage accounting, mandatory retention, monotonic logical positions, and
retention-one reconstruction. Runtime invariants protect cloned draft state,
next-token position, single re-decode, post-repair budgets, request isolation,
and explicit rejection of unsupported kernels. CPU property tests cover random
shapes and synthetic cases; real checkpoints and serving engines require
separate GPU gates.

Transition: I will now show how those abstractions map into the software stack.


SLIDE 29 — Implementation Architecture
Purpose: Present the package organization and provenance path.

Speaker notes:
The front end is YAML plus CLI. The evaluation registry emits raw JSONL and
Parquet aggregates, while manifests and structured logs preserve provenance.
Model adapters expose cache layout, queries, modalities, and logical positions.
The algorithm modules implement forecast through repair, and backend modules
provide explicit HF decoding plus serving wrappers. The common environment is
Python 3.11, Torch 2.8 with CUDA 12.8, Transformers 4.55.2, and FlashAttention
2.8.3. Deterministic ledgers, failed-row preservation, and resume by run and
sample ID support reproducible evaluation.

Transition: The Hugging Face runtime is the most instrumentable reference path.


SLIDE 30 — Hugging Face Runtime: Explicit Prefill and Decode
Purpose: Explain why the compressed path does not use model.generate().

Speaker notes:
The runtime prepares multimodal inputs, executes prefill explicitly, captures
K/V, query projections, and modality mappings, constructs the selected cache,
and then decodes one token at a time. This explicit loop is necessary because
the cache must be inspected and replaced between prefill and decode, and again
during repair. Hooks observe q_proj without modifying weights. Eager attention
is the complete instrumentation path. SDPA and FlashAttention-2 exact-decode
paths are enabled only after token-parity gates and remain labeled by their true
prefill and decode configuration.

Transition: Serving engines require a different kind of integration because
they own physical block allocation and scheduler metadata.


SLIDE 31 — Serving Backends: Real Native Transport, Narrow Support
Purpose: State what the vLLM and SGLang integrations do and do not prove.

Speaker notes:
The vLLM plugin can release whole physical blocks after prefill, update worker
and scheduler tables, construct compact attention metadata, and preserve logical
or multimodal RoPE positions. The SGLang patch compacts request-to-token
ownership, frees KV-pool slots, preserves logical positions, and fixes release
bookkeeping. Both paths fail closed for features that invalidate the transaction,
such as prefix or Radix caching, CUDA graphs, speculation, or unaudited kernels.
These are native physical-transport gates. Common-selector equivalence is still
pending, and serving prototypes and repair are unsupported.

Transition: Whether the backend is HF or a serving engine, scientific comparison
requires the same controlled protocol.


SLIDE 32 — Fair Evaluation Protocol
Purpose: Define apples-to-apples comparison and reporting rules.

Speaker notes:
Every direct comparison fixes prompt text, media, processor, chat template,
tokenization, output length, deterministic decoding, weights and revision,
precision, attention implementation, mandatory policy, and realized active-byte
budget. Timing uses the same warmups and synchronization boundaries, and every
row records software, GPU, seed, and failure status. We report task-local scores,
memory, phase-separated latency, fidelity diagnostics, and repair outcomes.
Incompatible benchmark metrics are not silently averaged, and synthetic values
never appear in measured tables.

Transition: With that protocol in mind, I will show what the current bounded
experiments establish—and what they do not.


SLIDE 33 — Exploratory GPU Pilot: What Was Compared
Purpose: Establish the pilot’s scope and passed invariants before showing scores.

Speaker notes:
The diagnostic pilot used Qwen2.5-VL-3B in BF16 with HF eager attention: twenty
examples each from MMStar, TextVQA, and ChartQA, four retention ratios, and 32
deterministic output tokens. All 1,740 rows completed. Retention-one identity,
monotonic active bytes, equal sample-level budgets, seeded random reproducibility,
complete timing, and revision recording passed. This is a clean-SHA diagnostic,
not a paper result. LOOK-M is unsupported on Qwen GQA, PrefixKV is generalized,
and pilot prototype/full labels used exact safety fallbacks.

Transition: The equal-budget scores revealed an important negative result.


SLIDE 34 — Pilot Baseline Comparison at Equal 20% Active KV Budget
Purpose: Present the early selector failure honestly and motivate the redesign.

Speaker notes:
At the same sample-level active bytes, Prompt Top-k preserved the FullKV TextVQA
score and reached 0.50 on ChartQA; generalized PrefixKV reached 0.72 and 0.65 on
those tasks. The early MosaicKV exact selector improved MMStar from 0.55 to 0.60
but collapsed to zero on TextVQA and ChartQA. This is not evidence for MosaicKV
superiority. It identified a real design bug: utility scales and evidence
allocation were not sufficiently modality balanced. The response was normalized
utility, explicit evidence reserves, and disjoint calibration and evaluation
ledgers.

Transition: A small held-out boundary check tested whether that selector fix
moved the memory frontier in the intended direction.


SLIDE 35 — Post-Pilot Selector Diagnostic: Same Quality at Half the Active Bytes
Purpose: Show the best current memory result without overstating it.

Speaker notes:
On five disjoint DocVQA examples, FullKV, Prompt Top-k at 20 percent, and
MosaicKV exact at 10 percent all scored five out of five. MosaicKV used 7.56 MB
versus 15.14 MB for Prompt Top-k, or 50.1 percent fewer active KV bytes at the
same score. Compression time was slightly lower for MosaicKV, but end-to-end
latency was one percent higher. This demonstrates a promising memory-frontier
point, not a statistically reliable quality win or a latency win. The run also
has dirty-tree provenance, so it cannot support a final paper table.

Transition: The ablations help explain which component changed block selection.


SLIDE 36 — Ablation: Forecasting Improves Block Recall
Purpose: Report forecast diagnostics and the gap between recall and task score.

Speaker notes:
In this bounded A100 ablation, hybrid forecasting improved mean selected-block
recall against the oracle by 0.2888 compared with no forecast. Forecast regret
also generally fell. However, task-score effects were inconsistent: DocVQA
improved, ChartQA declined, and MMStar and Video-MME did not change. This is an
important distinction. Forecasting can identify more oracle-selected blocks
without guaranteeing that the selected evidence produces a better final answer.
The sample size is tiny, so the result supports mechanism diagnosis only.

Transition: Graph, value utility, and prototypes show similarly nuanced effects.


SLIDE 37 — Ablation: Graph, Value Utility, and Prototypes
Purpose: Summarize the current component-level evidence, including mixed results.

Speaker notes:
The graph increased facility coverage, but task scores did not change on this
small slice, so we cannot claim a structured-task advantage. Removing value
awareness increased attention-output error by 0.0023, which supports the intended
local-fidelity role, but reduced logit KL by 0.0085, so the fidelity metrics are
mixed. At equal active bytes, measured phase-safe prototypes substantially
reduced attention-output error and logit KL relative to exact-only rows, while
task scores tied. The safe conclusion is that prototypes improved internal
fidelity here, not demonstrated benchmark quality.

Transition: Repair gives one concrete example where internal risk detection did
recover an answer, but its cost remains high.


SLIDE 38 — Repair Case Study and Policy Trade-off
Purpose: Show a representative successful repair and aggregate costs.

Speaker notes:
For ChartQA example 1447, FullKV answered 2980.2. The compressed no-repair path
dropped a digit and answered 298.6. Guarded prototype-risk repair restored image
blocks, re-decoded the current token, and answered 2980.05, recovering the task
score. Across seven examples, the score sum improved from four to five, with no
incorrect answer changes. But only one in four triggers was beneficial, mean
repair latency was 341 milliseconds, and paired end-to-end cost increased by
3.983 seconds. Lower-precision residuals halved storage but did not lower
latency because re-decode and dequantization dominated the small transfer.

Transition: That overhead is part of a broader systems question: when do fewer
KV bytes actually translate into faster inference?


SLIDE 39 — Systems Microbenchmark: Memory and Compression Overhead
Purpose: Present the systems campaign and its main lesson.

Speaker notes:
The bounded A100 campaign contains 440 per-trial rows, with five warmups and
twenty timed repetitions per point. The plots relate decode latency to active
memory and isolate compression overhead. The central result is that reducing
active bytes is necessary but not sufficient for speed. Forecasting, graph
construction, Python-side selection, and cache packing can dominate, especially
for short outputs. These measurements use a controlled synthetic visual
workload and a staged patch, so they are engineering diagnostics rather than
paper-eligible performance results.

Transition: Two implementation changes show that the decode path itself can
benefit once packing overhead is controlled.


SLIDE 40 — What Reduced Decode Latency—and What Still Dominates
Purpose: Identify measured speedups and the remaining bottleneck.

Speaker notes:
At identical active bytes and 64 output tokens, the dense exact fast path reduced
MosaicKV p50 decode from the Prompt Top-k value of 3.396 seconds to 3.338 seconds.
The changes were vectorized layer gathers, dense all-valid cache handling, and
append-only decode storage. At a smaller cache, eager prefill followed by SDPA
decode reduced MosaicKV p50 decode by 10.5 percent and end-to-end time by 7.6
percent. Yet at the matched Prompt Top-k comparison, MosaicKV still paid 0.778
seconds of compression overhead and lost end to end. Forecast and graph planning
are therefore the next optimization target.

Transition: Before interpreting backend performance, we also validate numerical
parity and identity.


SLIDE 41 — Backend Correctness: Reference and FullKV Paths
Purpose: Separate validated reference paths from compressed native execution.

Speaker notes:
HF eager is the instrumented reference. The SDPA and FlashAttention-2 exact
decode paths passed their listed token-parity gates and retention-one identity,
with eager prefill retained where scoring requires instrumentation. vLLM FullKV
matched HF for 16 tokens with prefix caching disabled. SGLang FullKV matched the
HF-SDPA reference for 32 tokens on its Torch-native path. These serving rows
validate deterministic FullKV wrappers; they are not compressed MosaicKV rows.
Native publication requires physical compaction and selector integration to pass
with logical positions, bytes, and request isolation together.

Transition: The next slide states exactly which native transaction properties
have already passed.


SLIDE 42 — Native Physical Compaction: What Is Proven
Purpose: Report native transport validation without implying method completion.

Speaker notes:
Both the installed vLLM and SGLang transport paths reduced an 18-block request
to nine blocks, preserved retention-one identity and logical positions, and
passed repeated selection plus A-B-A request-isolation tests. In the measured
A100 gates, active cache bytes fell from 11,132,928 to 5,824,512 per request at
retention 0.5. What remains pending is the end-to-end common MosaicKV selector
bridge and selection-equivalence proof. These are genuine native physical
transactions, but they are not yet native MosaicKV method results, and no
simulated row is shown.

SLIDE 43 — How the Design Addresses the Research Problem
Purpose: Synthesize the problem-to-mechanism argument.

Speaker notes:
Each design element addresses a specific failure mode. Forecasting addresses
future demand that prompt scores cannot see. The evidence graph preserves linked
multimodal chains. Value-aware utility accounts for attention-output behavior.
Facility coverage and prototypes reduce redundancy. Residual repair provides a
bounded response to rare high-risk errors. Logical-position maps and phase-safe
transforms protect RoPE correctness under physical packing. Finally,
phase-separated timing makes the systems break-even point explicit. MosaicKV is
therefore a coordinated design, not merely a new ranking score.

Transition: The same mapping also makes the current gaps easy to state.


SLIDE 44 — Current Limitations and Negative Findings
Purpose: Make limitations visible before the conclusion.

Speaker notes:
Most component evidence comes from only one to seven examples and sometimes a
dirty worktree. The early selector failed OCR and chart tasks. Graph coverage
has not yet produced a measured structured-task score advantage. Repair has low
precision and material end-to-end cost. On the systems side, planning can erase
decode savings; HF scoring still begins in eager prefill; common-selector
equivalence for vLLM and SGLang is pending; and native serving prototypes and
repair remain unsupported. These are explicit engineering and experimental
targets, not exclusions hidden from the comparison.

Transition: The next slide turns those limitations into concrete publication
gates.


SLIDE 45 — What Must Pass Before a Paper-Scale Claim?
Purpose: Define the checklist for scientifically defensible results.

Speaker notes:
Paper-scale evidence begins from a clean immutable commit and pinned model and
dataset revisions. Every model and backend must pass retention-one parity. Every
comparison must match sample-level bytes, prompts, media, precision, and output
length. Calibration and evaluation examples must remain disjoint. Results must
appear per model and task with paired confidence intervals before any macro
summary. Systems points require warmups and repeated synchronized trials.
Native rows need common-selector equivalence, failures must remain visible, and
the paper must show regimes where MosaicKV is not Pareto-optimal.

Transition: With those boundaries, I can summarize the contribution and the
current state in one slide.


SLIDE 46 — Takeaways
Purpose: Deliver the central contribution, evidence, and open problem.

Speaker notes:
MosaicKV reframes multimodal KV compression as forecasted, structured,
value-aware, budgeted, and recoverable evidence selection. Algorithmically, it
combines future-query forecasts, sparse evidence graphs, and submodular block
selection. Its representation uses exact blocks, phase-safe prototypes, and
indexed residuals. Its runtime uses explicit decoding, bounded repair, exact
byte accounting, and fail-closed backend support. Tiny held-out diagnostics show
that it can preserve quality at much smaller active-memory points and can
slightly improve decode latency after dense packing. Planning and repair cost,
as well as paper-scale generalization, remain open.

Transition: I will close with the design trade-off that should guide discussion.


SLIDE 47 — Discussion
Purpose: Invite questions around the core systems trade-off.

Speaker notes:
The central question is not whether compression can remove more tokens. It is
how much forecast quality and evidence structure are worth paying for in
planning cost, how aggressively active memory should be reduced, and how much
repair risk and residual storage are acceptable. Different workloads may choose
different points: short answers may favor simple exact pruning, while long
decodes or reusable plans may amortize richer selection. I welcome questions on
the algorithm, correctness, backend integration, or experimental design.

Transition: The remaining slides are appendix material for technical questions.


SLIDE 48 — Appendix: Attention Fidelity and Forecast Regret
Purpose: Provide a mathematical explanation for key/value scoring and forecast
diagnostics. Use on demand.

Speaker notes:
The first bound is a local perturbation view of attention. Errors in both K and V
contribute to attention-output error, which motivates measuring addressability
and value reconstruction instead of attention mass alone. The second expression
decomposes regret into error from forecasting Q and error from selecting under
that forecast. Future-query cosine and attention-rank diagnostics probe the
first term; exhaustive tiny-graph comparisons probe the selector term. These
are explanatory bounds and diagnostics, not a claim of a global tight guarantee
for the complete neural network.


SLIDE 49 — Appendix: Submodularity and the Systems Break-Even Point
Purpose: Explain selection assumptions and when compression can be faster. Use
on demand.

Speaker notes:
Lazy greedy has its familiar approximation behavior only when the registered
coverage terms are monotone submodular and the utility is compatible with those
assumptions. The tests therefore check monotonicity and diminishing returns only
in valid configurations and compare tiny instances with brute force. The
systems inequality is equally important: forecast, graph, selection, packing,
and repair must cost less than the decode time they save. Longer outputs, larger
visual prefixes, batching, or reusable plans improve amortization; short outputs
can rationally favor Prompt Top-k or another simpler method.
